**Ascend: short practice and an independent Playground**

The owner's 2026-11-02 follow-up asks for broader open-source lab research and on-demand execution on learners' PCs, with Linux first. See [the repository shortlist and learning paths](open-source-lab-research.md) and [the local execution, storage, and verification plan](local-lab-execution-plan.md). The shared catalog and seven runnable local recipes are implemented alongside the browser activities below; the [runner deployment guide](server-lab-runner.md) identifies remaining runtime requirements.

Approved design, 2026-11-02. Stages 1 and 2 and the implementable stage 3 milestones are implemented in the working tree; the canonical user/developer guides describe the resulting behavior. This document retains the research and acceptance rationale. The catalog now covers cybersecurity, Python and 3D reasoning; feedback-aware review and authored track checkpoints are implemented. Further learner timing/transfer sessions and a two-week observation window remain empirical follow-up work. See [the usability follow-up](practice-playground-ux.md) for the owner’s feedback and the dedicated activity/walkthrough design. Scope includes the owner's clarifications: both short practice and standalone challenges/free experimentation outside the regular track, with mystery and discovery formats that encourage investigation and reasoning. Mystery must be calibrated to challenge difficulty and the learner's relevant background. Teach through separate worked examples, then offer independent challenges; there is no general requirement to make each lab switchable into a guided version.

Keep one active learning track. Add a permanent **Playground** with **Quick practice**, **Challenges**, and **Free practice**. Playground activities have their own attempts and history, work without starting or switching a mission, and can cover subjects outside the active track. Today provides a short route into practice while keeping the learner's place on the main path visible.

The product promise should be: **“Keep your place in the track. Choose something that fits today.”** Finishing an exercise must not remove the ability to practice that skill again.

The combination that fits Ascend is a guided path plus an independent activity library, with recommendations connecting them. The cyber platforms contribute challenge discovery; Duolingo and Anki contribute ongoing practice; Codecademy contributes free experimentation. Merely adding several more side nodes would postpone the same failure. Requiring a second active track would make a brief detour unnecessarily expensive.

The follow-up reviews of beginner support and guidance scope used the linked TryHackMe difficulty/help articles, HTB Starting Point/Guided Mode/Challenges guides, PortSwigger preparation guide, and an ordinary lab's Solution disclosure, retrieved on 2026-11-02. The platforms use different combinations of teaching examples, optional solutions, and selective guidance. They neither establish a universal guided mode nor support claiming that no platform guides the same challenge. PortSwigger's preparation sequence is a recommendation; HTB Starting Point's tier progression is an explicit gate within that feature. None of these sources establishes automatic readiness detection for every learner.

Ascend's chosen policy is **worked example → fresh independent challenge → review**. A worked example may demonstrate a difficult technique step by step. An easy challenge must still ask the learner to apply an appropriate skill independently. Harder challenges retain their reasoning requirements. Difficulty describes the problem's complexity; assistance describes how an attempt was completed. Studying a hard solution does not turn that problem into an easy independent solve. This policy replaces the earlier proposal for an in-session guided-mode switch on starter mysteries.

The proposed experience has four clear places to do work.

| Place | Purpose and duration | What completion means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Track** | Follow the chosen curriculum; task estimates can span multiple working sessions. | Evidence-backed progress through prerequisites and milestones. |
| **Playground → Quick practice** | A bounded 5-, 10-, or 15-minute exercise/session. Default recommendations use familiar or relevant skills; the learner can select other supported topics. | A recorded practice result and useful feedback; another exercise is immediately available. |
| **Playground → Challenges** | Choose a standalone puzzle, investigation, or mini-project. Offer short, medium, and longer options, including 60–120-minute work where content supports it. | A challenge result in Playground history, independent of track completion. |
| **Playground → Free practice** | Experiment in a small workspace with no required finish line. Start from a fixture or blank supported template; save and return later. | Saved work and an optional substantive learning log. There is no automatic claim of passing an assessment. |

Playground belongs in primary navigation on desktop and mobile. Quick practice is a tab within it, also directly accessible from Today. All three Playground tabs work when a mission is active, paused, complete, or absent. Choosing an activity from another domain does not enroll the user in a track. “Related to your track” is a recommendation, not a restriction.

Today keeps the active track and its next task visible. A time choice influences the recommended action, rather than erasing the path. For example:

```text
Your track: Ethical Hacking Foundations
Next task: Investigate an HTTP service · about 90 minutes total
[Open track task]

Have 10 minutes?
Permission puzzle · a fresh case · no setup
[Start practice]  [Choose another]

[Browse Playground]
```

The title and times above are illustrative. If the next task has an authored short checkpoint, Today may also offer that checkpoint with its own estimate. Otherwise it should simply offer independent practice. It must not relabel an entire ninety-minute assessment as ten minutes.

After practice, show the result, the explanation, and three actions: **Another exercise**, **Back to track**, and **Finish for today**. A saved session receives a clear acknowledgment. No next-day wait is required. A challenge completion similarly offers another challenge or a return to the exact track position.

Ordinary challenge cards show topic, difficulty, estimated working time, setup requirements, device needs, verification type, and whether the item is new, started, or solved. Mystery cards use a neutral scenario title and understandable prerequisite abilities, withholding the diagnosis appropriate to their authored scope. Both retain honest duration, difficulty, setup, and device information. Label teaching content “Worked example” and independent tasks “Easy challenge,” “Intermediate challenge,” or “Hard challenge.” Where supplied, state whether a challenge offers limited hints; do not imply that every lab has a guided toggle. Filters cover duration, topic, difficulty, browser-only versus external setup, progress, and format. Provide search, bookmarks, and “Surprise me.” Browsing should work across supported subjects, not just the active pack. Prerequisite links lead to separate learning examples without forcing mission switching.

Free practice must provide something real to manipulate. Start with two small browser workspaces: an editable permission-policy/request sandbox, and a log/timeline sandbox where the learner changes filters or events and inspects the result. Include reset-to-fixture, save, and resume. A related existing local HTTP lab can be linked with setup instructions and saved notes, clearly labeled as running on the learner's machine. Broader code execution, hosted terminals, and automatically provisioned cyber ranges are later infrastructure work; they are not prerequisites for these two usable workspaces.

Mystery should be an authored learning format across the three Playground modes, available in the first complete release.

“Surprise me” chooses an activity. **Mystery** changes what the learner knows at the start and what they must discover. These are separate controls. The learner can deliberately choose a mystery, or let “Surprise me” choose one when mystery is among their preferred formats. This adds a format within the existing destinations, not a fourth destination or another progression track.

| Playground mode | Mystery flavor and example | Thinking the activity should elicit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quick practice** | A 5–10-minute mini-mystery: “One of these requests failed. What changed?” Supply a small set of requests, responses, and relevant context, without naming the faulty rule. | Make a prediction, choose one useful comparison, and identify the evidence supporting the cause. End with one contrasting case. Keep the investigation bounded enough to finish in a short session. |
| **Challenges** | A 15–60+ minute case: “A customer can see a document they should not see,” or “An unusual login appeared overnight.” Provide an app fixture or an evidence bundle, with the mechanism undisclosed. | Form competing hypotheses, choose tests, connect clues, rule out a plausible alternative, and support a conclusion. A staged case can reveal further evidence after a meaningful investigative action. |
| **Free practice** | An optional **Introduce an unknown fault** action on a separate copy of a workspace. The app chooses a supported hidden fault; the learner inspects behavior and tries to explain or repair it. | Design experiments, change one variable at a time, and compare before/after behavior. The learner can reveal the fault whenever useful and continue experimenting; free practice has no mandatory assessment finish line. |

Mystery depth should follow relevant preparation. **Teach the tools and concepts using an example; let the learner discover how they apply to a different case.** A learner with no knowledge of HTTP first needs an explanation and demonstration. Their first independent investigation should involve a small system and familiar concepts. The example and challenge have separate identities, fixtures, and results, even when offered consecutively in a short practice session.

Author problem difficulty through the required concepts, number of reasoning steps, number of plausible explanations, and complexity of the environment. A simple challenge can require independent thinking with one familiar mechanism. A hard challenge may combine several mechanisms and require the learner to design their own tests. Separately author shared worked examples that demonstrate prerequisite techniques, including difficult ones when useful. One good example can prepare learners for several challenges; neither a guided copy nor a complete walkthrough is required for every lab. The fresh challenge must change a meaningful condition or require a different decision, so copying the example's commands or answers is insufficient.

| Readiness for this subject | Default experience | What remains unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **New concept or background not yet established** | A separate short primer and worked example, followed by a fresh easy challenge when the needed abilities are established. The learning activity itself can fill today's session. | No required discovery in the teaching example; the fresh challenge asks for independent application. |
| **Concept introduced; application still developing** | A genuinely easy independent challenge: one familiar mechanism, a small evidence set, and a prepared environment. Return to a separate example if more teaching is needed. | Which taught explanation fits new evidence and what comparison supports it. |
| **Demonstrated independent application within a topic** | An independent mystery within that subject, with only the hints explicitly authored for it, if any. | The cause and which test will distinguish it from alternatives. |
| **Demonstrated application across the required subjects** | A mixed-topic investigation, limited to the supported skills and chosen difficulty. | The topic, mechanism, and sequence of useful tests. Setup and required working skills stay visible. |

These are content recommendations, not permanent learner ranks or another path. Being experienced in networking does not establish readiness for a web authorization mystery. Users may choose a mystery within a known subject or a **mixed-topic mystery**; a narrow topic filter necessarily reveals context, so the UI must not describe that experience as fully blind.

Use the following concrete readiness and recovery rules:

1. **Describe prerequisites per activity.** Authors specify the abilities needed, available prerequisite examples, and any limited hints or post-attempt review material. For an HTTP investigation, “read a response status” and “compare two requests” are useful prerequisites. “Level 5” or total XP is insufficient. A beginner's worked example usually introduces one idea at a time; the following easy challenge requires those already introduced abilities.
2. **Use evidence per skill, with uncertainty.** Relevant observed exercise results and changed-case checks can support a readiness estimate. Track completion, self-reported experience, a file existing, and reading a solution are weaker signals; none alone proves independent application. Retain assistance and reveal history. A walkthrough introduces a skill but cannot automatically qualify the learner for a blind assessment. Unknown history means readiness is unknown, not that the learner is incapable.
3. **Offer a short route for either background.** A newcomer gets the primer and separate demonstration. Someone who already knows the material can skip them and try a brief prerequisite check. Do not require enrollment, switching missions, or repeating already demonstrated work. The check must sample the actual required abilities; a vocabulary question cannot stand in for a multi-step practical skill. Inadequate evidence defaults recommendations toward preparation or a simpler independent challenge.
4. **Control recommendations before selection.** Resolve prerequisite readiness, problem difficulty, available time, and environment before adding an activity to Quick practice or “Surprise me.” Never automatically assign a blind mystery containing an unintroduced prerequisite. The full catalog remains browsable; a manually chosen stretch activity offers “Study an example,” “Choose an easier challenge,” or “Try this challenge.” Each choice preserves the original task's difficulty and does not fabricate readiness or change the active mission.
5. **Increase difficulty after demonstrated transfer.** As an initial, tunable rule, suggest a more demanding independent case after two distinct suitable cases are solved with the required evidence and without hints or solution reveals, including a changed-condition check. Readiness remains specific to those skills. Increase complexity in bounded steps; a single error does not erase prior progress. A returning learner can take a warm-up without restarting a track.
6. **Make getting unstuck part of the activity.** Offer prerequisite examples, a simpler independent exercise, a save-for-later action, and setup help. Show a hint action only when a limited hint has been authored for this challenge. Preserve the unsolved case and notes while the learner studies a separate example; never turn the current challenge into a walkthrough automatically. Repeated unsuccessful checks may offer these routes, but inactivity alone does not establish a skill gap. Setup failures are not evidence of conceptual failure.
7. **Include preparation in the time promise.** A ten-minute beginner session could contain a two-minute explanation, a two-minute demonstration of example A, and a six-minute independent challenge B; the durations need validation. If preparation takes longer, offer that learning activity with its real estimate or another suitable short exercise. Never advertise ten minutes and then require an unannounced hour of prerequisite work. A meaningful preparation session counts toward the habit without requiring the advanced mystery to be solved.

For example, a worked HTTP example can explain requests and response codes and demonstrate how to distinguish a missing page from a stopped service. A separate easy challenge then asks the learner to diagnose a fresh small case without step-by-step instructions. Later challenges can involve denied access, multiple requests, or several interacting components once those mechanisms have been introduced. A complex investigation may also have a dedicated teaching example elsewhere in the library; studying that example does not solve or simplify the active challenge.

This progression also applies to workspace faults: the first fault should concern a rule the learner has been shown in a separate example, with a working baseline and a resettable copy. Only introduce mixed or chained faults when their component skills are supported. Prerequisite examples and setup help remain useful even when a challenge intentionally offers no hints or walkthrough.

An effective mystery follows a compact reasoning loop: **observe → predict → test → explain → try a changed case**. Scale the loop to the activity. A five-minute puzzle might need only an evidence selection and one prediction; a longer investigation can support a notebook of hypotheses and test results. Avoid turning every short puzzle into an essay or a long checklist.

Use several authored formats to keep that loop varied: diagnose a hidden fault; determine which of two plausible explanations fits; predict the result of changing a condition; repair a failure while preserving a legitimate workflow; or investigate whether apparently suspicious evidence has a benign explanation. In longer cases, an initial plausible conclusion can be challenged by a new clue. Such a change must follow the scenario's established rules. Difficulty should come from reasoning about evidence, not obscure trivia, missing instructions, arbitrary traps, or a surprise change to the pass criterion.

Each scored mystery needs a stated finish line and an authored evidence rubric. A final label or flag alone should not satisfy a case whose objective is diagnosis: require a relevant evidence reference, a discriminating prediction/test result, or a passing repair with a control, as appropriate. Prefer checks that can actually be verified against the fixture. Free-form explanations can support reflection or human review, but their presence must not be reported as automatically verified reasoning. Where the evidence is intentionally inconclusive, “insufficient evidence” can be a valid result with a precise rationale in the rubric.

Challenge help is an explicit authoring policy. Independent challenges can offer **no hints** or a small number of **limited hints**, such as pointing to a useful observation. Hints are optional content, not a mandatory ladder ending in a full solution for every lab. Basic tool documentation and setup support remain available. Missing knowledge leads to a separate worked example; that example may teach an advanced technique step by step, with its own fixture and learning result.

Record any challenge-specific hints and distinguish an assisted solve from an independent solve without deducting points for requesting an available hint. Do not offer a bonus for fast guessing. If an authored full solution is available, expose it as a distinct post-attempt review: the learner solves the challenge or explicitly ends the attempt before opening it. An ended/revealed case can be practiced later, but cannot retroactively earn a new first-solve award or qualify as a fresh blind assessment. Retain reveal history across refreshes and across any workspace sharing the same fixture. Learning from examples and reviewing solutions can earn habit credit; independent readiness must still be demonstrated on a fresh case.

The debrief explains why the conclusion follows, which clue mattered, why a plausible alternative fails, and what would change under a different condition. Offer a short follow-up with that condition changed. This makes the reveal a learning event and checks whether the reasoning transfers beyond remembering the answer.

Preserve the unknown appropriate to the challenge's authored scope until a permitted hint or post-attempt review reveals it. Public titles, tags, recommendations, URLs, filenames, accessible labels, and API metadata should not accidentally announce the diagnosis or expose the answer key. A separate worked example deliberately explains its own mechanism; recommendations and example titles must not implicitly diagnose the active mystery. Never obscure prerequisite, environment, or scope instructions just to preserve surprise. Separate the scenario brief from private solution/rubric data; do not just hide the solution label with CSS. Save each run's fixture, discovered clues, hint/reveal state, and work so resuming preserves the same case. If the learner already solved or revealed that case, label it as familiar practice; use another suitable case when promising a blind investigation. Cosmetic renaming is not a new mystery.

Repeatability needs an explicit content and selection model.

1. **Separate the exercise definition from each attempt.** A permission skill can produce many practice runs while the original track assessment remains final. Each new run has its own fixture, content version, result, hints used, and timestamps. Reviewing an old answer is labeled review; it is not presented as a new solve.
2. **Reuse existing generators where they produce meaningful variation.** Native families already include access policies, subnets, logging, integrity, and timelines. Change the inputs and required reasoning, include contrasting cases, and test that generated instances remain valid. Static `case` exercises currently only shuffle the same two answers; shuffling them is not new content. Those need authored variants or an honest review label.
3. **Offer a default session, not a large to-do list.** Match prerequisite readiness and difficulty along with the requested duration, device constraints, and preferred format. Then recommend a fresh variant of a recently missed skill, a due review, or a relevant less-practiced skill. Use recent attempt and solution-reveal history to rotate topics and avoid immediate duplicates or spoiled mysteries. Unfamiliar skills lead to a separate teaching example or preparation session before independent testing. The learner can always choose another topic or the full catalog.
4. **Keep practice available when nothing is due.** Start with simple review intervals of 1, 3, 7, and 14 days, adjusted by recorded performance; treat these as tunable product defaults, not a validated mastery model. When no review is due, draw from fresh familiar-skill variants or the starter pool. Exhausting finite authored scenarios leads to clearly labeled review or another skill, not fabricated “new” questions.
5. **Make cold starts and sparse catalogs usable.** Bundle prerequisite-light starter exercises with the app. At least two beginner browser-only practice options must remain available without completing a milestone or installing another track. If a narrow topic/device filter has no matching content, explain that and offer named alternatives. Do not silently substitute a long lab.
6. **Make duration a content promise that is checked.** Include necessary setup in the displayed estimate, or show setup separately before starting. A five-minute session must have a useful stopping point designed for that size. A timer alone does not turn a large challenge into short practice. Save incomplete work when a learner takes longer.

Practice, challenge results, habit credit, and curriculum progress need distinct rules.

| Activity | Habit/streak | XP and result policy | Track effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary track work | Existing substantive partial logs continue to count. | Existing evidence and scoring rules remain. | Only its actual completion changes node state. |
| Quick practice | A verified finished exercise can produce one habit event; a failed/incomplete attempt can count through a substantive progress log, consistent with existing partial work. Respect the existing automatic-streak setting. | Proposed launch rule: 10 practice XP for a successful fresh run, at most once per exercise family per logical day, capped at 30 practice XP per day. Practice remains unlimited after the reward cap. | No milestone unlock, node reset, or main-path percentage increase. |
| Standalone challenge | A solve or substantive partial log counts. | A fixed authored first-solve award for a verified challenge, at most once per stable challenge reward key. Initial difficulty bands can use 25/50/100 XP. Replays preserve the first result and earn no repeat first-solve award. | No automatic equivalence to a track milestone. |
| Free practice | Saving substantive learning notes counts. Simply opening a workspace or leaving a timer running does not. | No automatic verified XP. Saved work is shown in activity history. | No curriculum-state changes. |

The new XP amounts are proposed launch defaults, not conclusions from competitor research. Display track, practice, and challenge contributions in Stats so total XP is not mistaken for curriculum completion. Keep every existing award and result. If the same underlying challenge is exposed in both places, share a stable first-solve reward key so copying or renaming it cannot award the same achievement twice. Small capped practice credit remains distinct from that first-solve reward. Completion and reward writes must be atomic and idempotent.

Use the existing configured timezone, 04:00 logical-day rollover, freezes, and reminder-suppression behavior. A Playground log needs an explicit activity association and no implicit active-mission association. `mission_id=None` passed into today's logging path is insufficient because that path defaults to the active mission.

Long track tasks also need an honest working-session flow. Keep partial progress and a concrete next action easy to save. Add authored checkpoints where they make educational sense, without replacing a verified parent's finish line. Save-and-resume pauses worked examples and free workspaces. Assessed quick exercises and standalone challenges save work with a continuous clock until completion or explicit end; returning keeps the original start. Attempt history groups activities without merging their individual work or results. Existing scored track attempts retain their current clock and final-result rules. Browsing Playground never ends one. If a scored attempt is running, starting another activity requires an explicit “End scored attempt and switch” action that explains the consequence; never silently pause/reset its score or discard its work. Improving pausing for scored assessments would be a separate scoring decision, not a hidden side effect of adding Playground.

Before release, validate enough prerequisite-light, browser-only options to exercise the no-empty-state promise. Acceptance should focus on the reported failure and the clarified independent-playground requirement:

1. Complete the only currently eligible ten-minute side puzzle while the next main task exceeds ten minutes. Select ten minutes again. A runnable practice activity appears, and the main task remains visible.
2. Finish multiple practice sessions on the same day and across subsequent days. The next session remains available; avoid the same fixture immediately; reaching an XP cap does not block practice.
3. Start with no completed nodes, no active mission, or a fully completed track. Starter practice and Playground browsing still work.
4. Start and save a challenge from a different topic without changing the active mission, task cursor, prerequisite states, or main-path percentage.
5. Use both free-practice workspaces: change inputs, observe results, save, reload, resume, and reset a copy without losing the saved original.
6. Keep the original permission/case assessment receipt, clock, and XP exactly as they were. New practice cannot turn an incorrect track attempt into a passed one.
7. Retry a submission, refresh after completion, and submit simultaneously from two clients. Each run gets one final result and one applicable award. Cross-user run IDs and workspace IDs cannot expose or modify another user's work.
8. Exhaust the due-review queue and finite authored variants. Show available fresh practice or labeled review; never pretend a renamed answer is new content.
9. Check short-time and device filters against actual setup. A phone-only five-minute choice must not launch a terminal or Docker task. Narrow filters with no matches offer explicit alternatives.
10. Save substantive Playground work around the configured day rollover. Streaks and reminders use existing day rules, while raw timer time and page visits give no credit.
11. Browse Playground during a scored track attempt, then try to start practice. Browsing changes nothing; switching requires an explicit action and retains the original score semantics. Two concurrent starts cannot create two active foreground sessions.
12. Exercise the complete flow through the browser and shared CLI/API, including keyboard/mobile layouts, paused work, server restart, and migration from an existing profile. Preserve private pack edits and stable task IDs.
13. Choose a mini-mystery and a longer mixed-topic mystery. Verify that each has enough evidence to solve within its declared background and estimated scope, and that naming a diagnosis alone cannot bypass its required evidence check.
14. Inspect mystery discovery cards, detail responses, resource filenames, routes, and accessible labels before solving. They reveal necessary requirements but not the intended diagnosis or private answer rubric. A topic-filtered or previously revealed case is never advertised as fully blind.
15. Exercise both none/limited hint policies and optional post-attempt review. An authored full solution is available only after solving or explicitly ending the attempt; preserve assistance and reveal state through refreshes. A revealed case cannot later earn a new first-solve award. The debrief offers a meaningful changed case, while practice remains available after help.
16. Introduce an unknown fault into a copy of a saved workspace, investigate it, and reveal/reset it. Preserve the original work and verify that the repaired scenario still supports its intended legitimate behavior. Validate generated mysteries against their authored evidence rubric, including contrasting and benign cases where supplied.
17. With no skill history, choose ten-minute practice and “Surprise me.” Offer a preparation session or a worked example followed by a distinct suitable easy challenge within the stated time. Never assign an unsupported blind case. Manually opening advanced content offers preparation, a simpler independent task, or a deliberate attempt without rewriting the selected lab.
18. Give a learner high XP or progress in an unrelated domain. Confirm that this does not qualify them for a mystery in an unfamiliar subject. Conversely, relevant demonstrated skills can qualify a learner without enrolling them in or completing another track.
19. Leave an unsolved hard mystery to study a prerequisite example, then return. Preserve the hard case's fixture, difficulty, requirements, and notes; it remains incomplete and has no automatic guided-mode conversion. Report an environment problem without recording a conceptual failure. A solved example alone cannot promote the learner to independent readiness.
20. Complete suitable fresh cases independently and offer a bounded increase in complexity. Preparation time stays in the estimate and readiness remains an explicitly limited per-skill estimate. Pilot beginner sessions with someone unfamiliar with the topic to check that instruction in example A prepares them to reason through challenge B without copying the demonstrated solution. A limited-hint or no-hint lab must remain usable without authoring a step-by-step counterpart.

After release, review a small set of local usage measures over roughly two weeks: short-session availability, setup-adjusted completion time, unwanted repetition, saved work successfully resumed, and whether the learner can return to the track without confusion. For mysteries, also inspect where hints were needed, whether evidence checks were met, and how learners handle a changed follow-up case; clicks or time spent alone do not establish learning. Ask whether discoveries feel satisfying and difficulties feel fair. Track advancement every day is not the success condition; useful learning should remain possible on both busy days and days chosen for exploration.
